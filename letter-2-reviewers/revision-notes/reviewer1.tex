\begin{ReviewerComment}
The manuscript presents an ambitious framework that integrates surrogate modeling and deep reinforcement learning for urban morphology and energy optimization. However, several critical issues currently limit the robustness and interpretability of the reported findings. Substantial changes are required before the manuscript can be considered for publication.
\end{ReviewerComment}
%
\begin{Answer}
We sincerely thank the Reviewer for the careful reading and for the constructive and technically demanding comments. We agree that the original manuscript was too ambitious in the way it framed the DRL results. In the revised manuscript, we therefore reworked both the experimental protocol and the narrative. In particular, the current paper no longer claims that DDPG outperforms NSGA-II. Instead, it presents a matched-checkpoint comparison, reports the negative result that fair-budget NSGA-II remains stronger than DDPG on the matched remote surrogate checkpoint, and discusses the resulting methodological implications and limitations explicitly. We respond to each comment point by point below.
\end{Answer}

\begin{ReviewerComment}
1. The study relies exclusively on simulated outputs to generate the dataset for surrogate modeling and DRL optimization, without any empirical or independent external validation to assess the realism of the simulation results.
\end{ReviewerComment}
%
\begin{Answer}
We agree with this concern. We could not add a full physical-stack or empirical validation workflow in the current revision, so we chose not to overstate the practical realism of the surrogate-generated results. Instead, we revised the paper in three ways.

First, we explicitly state in the Abstract, Discussion, and Conclusions that both optimization and independent re-evaluation remain surrogate-bound and still rely on the fallback analytic simulator. This limitation is now part of the core interpretation of the paper rather than a minor caveat.

Second, we strengthened the independent verification within the currently available pipeline. Appendix~B now reports an independent re-evaluation of the matched-checkpoint selected DDPG and NSGA-II candidates using a deterministic follow-up simulation call, so that the paper no longer relies solely on optimization-time surrogate outputs.

Third, we reframed the paper's contribution accordingly. The manuscript is no longer presented as a validated real-world co-optimization study. It is now framed as a methodological benchmarking study under surrogate approximators, with explicit discussion of the consequences of this limitation for the interpretation of all comparative results.
\end{Answer}
\begin{RevisionExcerpt}
\noindent\textbf{Revised Abstract text:}\\
``The framework should therefore be interpreted as a methodological case study in surrogate-conditioned region-finding and comparative fragility rather than as evidence of robust preference-articulated Pareto exploration or benchmark superiority. Because optimization and independent re-evaluation still rely on a fallback analytic simulator, because the matched-candidate DDPG re-evaluation shows much larger EUIt drift and larger EG discrepancy than the selected NSGA-II counterparts, and because benchmark outcomes are surrogate-checkpoint-dependent, the reported findings should be interpreted with substantial caution.''
\end{RevisionExcerpt}

\begin{ReviewerComment}
2. The claimed superiority of the DRL framework over NSGA-II may be methodologically biased, as the utopian-point-based reward explicitly guides the search toward a predefined ideal region, whereas NSGA-II follows a standard Pareto-based formulation emphasizing diversity.
\end{ReviewerComment}
%
\begin{Answer}
We agree, and this point became one of the main motivations for the revised benchmark design. We addressed the concern in both the method description and the experimental comparison.

At the methodological level, we now describe the reward explicitly as a scalarized weighted-distance objective around a utopian point, not as a direct Pareto-learning mechanism. In the reward subsection of the manuscript, the current text now makes clear that the weighted-distance reward is a preference-encoding device and that the corresponding conclusions should be interpreted as scalarized search behaviour rather than as an intrinsic Pareto superiority claim.

At the experimental level, we no longer claim that DRL is superior to NSGA-II. After completing a matched remote-checkpoint comparison with equal budgets across DDPG, random search, and fair-budget NSGA-II, the main result is now the opposite of the earlier optimistic story: fair-budget NSGA-II is stronger than DDPG, whereas DDPG remains only modestly stronger than same-budget random search. This change directly removes the unsupported superiority claim that the Reviewer correctly challenged.
\end{Answer}
\begin{RevisionExcerpt}
\noindent\textbf{Revised benchmark text:}\\
``After establishing the internal behaviour of the DRL agents, we compare them to the NSGA-II benchmark on a matched remote surrogate checkpoint. Table~\ref{tab:perf} summarises this apples-to-apples comparison. On this matched result set, NSGA-II attains the strongest Pareto-quality metrics (HV~=~1.331, IGD~=~0.086), while the three DDPG scenarios are clearly weaker (HV~=~0.470--0.742, IGD~=~0.501--0.618). A same-budget matched random-search ablation is weaker than the DDPG scenarios, but it also remains clearly weaker than NSGA-II.'' 
\end{RevisionExcerpt}

\begin{ReviewerComment}
3. The surrogate model is trained on only 500 samples in a 12-dimensional continuous design space, which may be sufficient for regression but is likely insufficient to robustly support surrogate-guided reinforcement learning optimization, particularly in extreme or sparsely sampled regions.
\end{ReviewerComment}
%
\begin{Answer}
We agree. The revision does not try to argue that 500 samples are fully sufficient for strong optimization claims. Instead, we now make this limitation explicit and document its consequences.

In the main manuscript, Section~2 now states clearly that the surrogate is trained on 500 generated morphologies in a 12-dimensional design space, and the Discussion reiterates that this scale is limited for optimization in sparse or extreme regions. In Appendix~A, we also consolidated the surrogate-validation material, including the five-fold cross-validation summary, an additional surrogate-fidelity audit, and error diagnostics in extreme regions of the target distributions.

Most importantly, the revised results now show that standard fit quality alone is not enough: two surrogate checkpoints with similar in-sample fit statistics can still produce different optimizer rankings. We therefore treat the 500-sample issue not as a solved concern, but as part of the paper's main methodological message: under limited data, optimization conclusions can be fragile and checkpoint-dependent even when conventional surrogate-validation metrics appear acceptable.
\end{Answer}
\begin{RevisionExcerpt}
\noindent\textbf{Revised methodology text:}\\
``This parametric generation process is controlled by twelve selected UMFs \ldots In the present dataset, 500 generated morphologies are used for surrogate training and validation; this sample size is sufficient for an initial regression study but remains limited relative to the full 12-dimensional continuous design space.''\\[0.5em]
\noindent\textbf{Revised limitations text:}\\

\end{RevisionExcerpt}

\begin{ReviewerComment}

\end{ReviewerComment}
%
\begin{Answer}
We agree and revised the introduction accordingly. The updated Introduction now links the study more directly to low-carbon urban energy design and to the practical difficulty of running large comparative sweeps with expensive simulation workflows. We also added an explicit explanation of why a surrogate-bound methodological study remains relevant for \emph{Applied Energy}: in energy-system design practice, surrogate-assisted optimization is often used as a screening layer before full physics validation, so understanding benchmark fragility under surrogate approximators is itself an energy-systems methodological question. This revised framing shifts the paper away from a narrow algorithm-improvement narrative and toward a more domain-relevant methodological contribution.
\end{Answer}
\begin{RevisionExcerpt}
\noindent\textbf{Revised Introduction text:}\\
``This context also highlights the methodological importance of surrogate-based design studies. Under these conditions, optimizer reliability becomes part of the energy-design problem itself: if a learning-based method is unstable, checkpoint-sensitive, or difficult to benchmark fairly, its practical value for urban energy design is limited.''
\end{RevisionExcerpt}

\begin{ReviewerComment}
6. Given the 12-dimensional continuous action space, the training duration (200 episodes $\\times$ 40 steps) appears relatively short. Stronger evidence or justification is required to demonstrate that the DRL agents have adequately converged.
\end{ReviewerComment}
%
\begin{Answer}
We agree. The revised manuscript no longer relies on the earlier short-horizon presentation. Instead, the current paper reports multi-seed 600-episode guarded reruns as the main source of training-dynamics evidence. We also completed a matched-checkpoint early-stopping variant at 350 episodes.

The key point is that the revised interpretation is conservative. We do not claim asymptotic convergence. Rather, the updated text states that DDPG can reach high-performing surrogate regions but often fails to preserve them, as shown by frequent late-stage regression. The early-stopping experiments further show that some of this instability is attributable to overtraining after entering a strong region, but the remediation remains partial and does not recover NSGA-II-level performance.
\end{Answer}
\begin{RevisionExcerpt}
\noindent\textbf{Revised main-text discussion:}\\
``The frequent late-stage regression in DDPG indicates that the method can discover strong regions but often fails to preserve them once found. A completed 350-episode early-stopping variant reduces the late-regression fraction from 0.80 to 0.70 in the balanced scenario and from 0.90 to 0.55 in the saving-focused scenario, but the generation-focused scenario worsens slightly from 0.80 to 0.85. Yet these changes still do not translate into a clear objective-quality improvement over the full-length matched runs, which suggests that the shortcoming is structural rather than merely a consequence of overtraining.''
\end{RevisionExcerpt}
