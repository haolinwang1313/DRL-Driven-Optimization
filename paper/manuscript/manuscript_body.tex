\begin{abstract}
Block-scale urban energy design requires methods that relate urban morphology to energy demand, rooftop photovoltaic potential, and winter sunlight access while limiting the cost of simulation-based search. \rev{This study evaluates surrogate-conditioned optimizer ranking in a shared block morphology workflow. Feasible block morphologies are first generated and converted into 12 morphology descriptors. Energy Use Intensity, EUIt, Energy Generation, EG, and sunlight hours, H, are then assigned through an analytic demand-generation-sunlight response generator.} The selected DNN surrogate is trained on the 2000-sample regime identified from a nested 500/1000/1500/2000-sample scale study and used as the common environment for DDPG and NSGA-II. Repeated five-fold validation gives mean nMAE values of 0.0176, 0.0265, and 0.0145 for EUIt, EG, and H. \rev{In descriptor space, NSGA-II reaches near-ceiling HV under the fixed benchmark reference, while DDPG remains scenario dependent. Projection to generated feasible blocks compresses the NSGA-II archive from 2000 descriptor candidates to 51 unique feasible blocks and changes the interpretation of archive diversity.} \rev{A 24-case physics-based cross-model stress test shows weak metric agreement and unsupported ranking transfer, while a four-block cross-climate sensitivity analysis shows stable EG ordering but unstable H ordering and severe-cold EUIt ordering.} These findings indicate that surrogate-conditioned optimizer rankings are meaningful only when interpreted in relation to evaluation mode, projection distance, and climate sensitivity, rather than as direct feasible-space or physics-based superiority.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

\subsection{Motivation}

Urban energy planning increasingly requires early-stage design methods that connect block morphology with building demand, rooftop photovoltaic potential, and winter sunlight access. Morphology decisions made at the block scale constrain later energy-system choices because floor area, density, openness, roof exposure, and street-canyon geometry affect both demand-side and supply-side performance. This relationship is especially relevant in Chinese urban districts, where rapid renewal, distributed photovoltaic deployment, and climate-resilient planning must be considered before detailed building-system design \cite{ref1,ref2}.

Simulation-based urban energy modelling has made this connection more explicit, but full physical simulation remains expensive when thousands of candidate morphologies and repeated optimizer runs are required \cite{ref6,ref7}. Surrogate-assisted workflows are therefore useful as screening tools, provided that the surrogate data source, input representation, and evaluation mode are stated clearly. In the present study, the optimizer benchmark is not based on direct annual EnergyPlus and Radiance execution for all 2000 samples. The benchmark instead uses a controlled analytic demand-generation-sunlight response surface and then checks selected generated blocks through a limited physics-based cross-model stress test and a limited cross-climate sensitivity analysis.

The methodological problem is therefore not only whether the surrogate has low average prediction error. Once DDPG and NSGA-II search the same surrogate response surface, optimizer ranking can depend on descriptor coverage, output clipping, retained-output contracts, and the mapping from continuous descriptor outputs to generated feasible blocks. Consequently, a high descriptor-space score should not be read directly as feasible-block superiority or physics-based superiority. This study addresses that interpretation problem in a block-scale urban energy design setting.

\subsection{Literature review}

Urban building energy modelling provides the physical and computational basis for connecting morphology with energy performance. Bottom-up urban building energy models and geospatial building-energy frameworks have improved the representation of urban-scale demand, while recent reviews have clarified the assumptions and data requirements of these tools \cite{ref8,ref18}. At the same time, large-scale urban morphology studies have shown that spatial structure can explain important variation in building energy demand across cities and under extreme climate conditions \cite{wang2026unlocking,wang2026climateResilient}.

Optimization has also become a common method for early-stage energy-oriented urban design. NSGA-II and related evolutionary algorithms are widely used because they generate explicit trade-off archives for objectives such as energy demand, photovoltaic potential, and daylight access \cite{ref11,ref12}. However, a Pareto archive alone does not resolve how optimizer conclusions should be interpreted when the evaluation surface is a surrogate, nor does it show whether a retained descriptor-space archive remains diverse after projection to generated feasible morphologies \cite{ref13,ref22}.

Policy-learning methods offer a different way to search a continuous surrogate environment. DDPG can encode reward preferences and produce repeated descriptor queries, but this formulation should not be treated as a natural physical-time control problem in the present morphology setting \cite{ref26,ref32}. The environment serializes static surrogate queries: each action is an absolute normalized descriptor vector, and each episode is a fixed sequence of surrogate interactions. This distinction is central for comparing DDPG with direct continuous and Pareto-based optimizers.

Existing studies therefore leave a specific gap. Urban energy optimization has been widely studied, surrogate modelling has been widely used, and reinforcement learning has been widely applied in operational energy control. What remains less clear is how optimizer ranking should be interpreted when policy learning and Pareto search are compared on the same surrogate-conditioned morphology workflow, especially after feasible-block projection and limited physics-based sensitivity checks.

\subsection{Contributions and paper organization}

This study provides a bounded comparison of policy learning and Pareto search for surrogate-assisted block-scale urban energy design. The contributions are threefold.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
\item It constructs a feasible-morphology workflow in which generated block geometries are converted into 12 morphology descriptors and assigned EUIt, EG, and H values through an analytic demand-generation-sunlight response surface.
\item It compares DDPG and NSGA-II in the same guarded DNN surrogate environment under matched query budgets, while separating descriptor-space archives, equal-size retained outputs, and projected feasible-block representations.
\item It uses a 24-case physics-based cross-model stress test and a four-block cross-climate sensitivity analysis to delimit how surrogate-conditioned optimizer rankings should be interpreted for early-stage design support.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section~2 describes morphology generation, analytic response generation, surrogate modelling, DDPG, benchmark protocol, feasible projection, and sensitivity analysis. Section~3 presents the surrogate, optimizer, projection, physics-based, and climate results. Section~4 discusses the implications and limitations. Section~5 concludes the paper. Supplementary Information provides case settings, response equations, validation details, optimizer analyses, physical case results, and climate case results.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

\rev{This section describes the workflow in the order required for reproduction. The procedure first generates feasible block morphologies and derives morphology descriptors. It then assigns analytic EUIt, EG, and H targets, trains the selected DNN surrogate, compares DDPG and NSGA-II within the shared surrogate environment, and maps optimizer outputs back to generated feasible blocks for physical and climate sensitivity analysis. This ordering is important because the optimizer operates in descriptor space, whereas the post-optimization analyses operate on generated block geometries. Figure~\ref{fig:workflow} summarizes the resulting information flow.}

\subsection{Feasible morphology generation and descriptor construction}

The morphology generator first constructs feasible block layouts and only then calculates descriptor values. Each block is represented as a 240~m $\times$ 240~m square divided into a $3\times3$ grid of 80~m $\times$ 80~m land units. One cell is assigned as open space, and the remaining eight cells are populated with residential prototypes. For each generated block, the generator samples the open-space position, building prototype, number of floors, and orientation angle. The orientation angle is sampled within -45~deg to 45~deg. The resulting block is therefore a generated feasible morphology, while the 12 input quantities used by the surrogate are descriptors derived from that morphology.

The descriptor set includes FAR, SD, AF, AR\_ew, AR\_ns, SVF, BD, OSR, SC, PAR, theta, and OSLI. These quantities are not 12 independent design variables. For example, FAR, BD, AF, and OSR are linked through the same footprint and gross-floor-area quantities, while OSLI is an integer descriptor of open-space position. Consequently, optimization results are reported as descriptor-space candidates until they are projected back to generated feasible blocks.

\subsection{Analytic response generation}

The target values in the optimizer benchmark come from an analytic response generator rather than from direct annual EnergyPlus and Radiance execution.
The analytic mapping converts the 12 morphology descriptors to EUIt, EG, and H through explicit demand, rooftop-generation, and sunlight-access functions. These functions preserve the intended directional interpretation of the original physics-based workflow: lower EUIt is preferred, while higher EG and H are preferred. The active 2000-sample regime is a nested prefix of the maximum generated morphology pool and uses simulation\_mode = fallback\_analytic.

This choice makes the benchmark reproducible and computationally stable, but it also defines the main interpretation boundary of the paper. The surrogate and optimizer comparison assess behaviour on a controlled analytic response surface. They do not replace full annual physical simulation of every generated morphology. The Dongtai station has zero fallback bias in the analytic generator, while non-Dongtai fallback bias is not used for the main benchmark. Supplementary Section S1 gives the full equations, station-bias convention, Gaussian-noise terms, and clipping ranges of 66.0--96.0 for EUIt, 1.2--2.85 for EG, and 6.0--7.85 for H.

\subsection{Surrogate model training and robustness assessment}

The selected surrogate is assessed through repeated five-fold cross-validation, leave-one-OSLI-out validation, outer-shell holdout, and feature-tail holdout. The repeated five-fold protocol evaluates interpolation performance under randomized folds. The leave-one-OSLI-out protocol tests whether the surrogate remains stable when one open-space-position class is withheld. The outer-shell and feature-tail holdouts stress the descriptor-domain boundary without claiming true out-of-distribution generalization.

The selected DNN comes from the 2000-sample regime of the nested scale study. The round-2 assessment keeps the model-comparison role separate from the optimizer-comparison role: the scale study identifies the surrogate checkpoint, while the robustness checks report how accurately that checkpoint reproduces the analytic response-generation regime. The repeated cross-validation mean nMAE values are 0.0176 for EUIt, 0.0265 for EG, and 0.0145 for H, with corresponding Spearman correlations of 0.991, 0.984, and 0.995.

\subsection{DDPG as serialized static black-box search}

The DDPG environment serializes repeated queries to a static guarded surrogate. At the beginning of each episode, a random descriptor query is evaluated to construct the initial normalized three-target state. At each subsequent step, the actor outputs an absolute 12-dimensional normalized descriptor action. This action is not an incremental modification of the preceding block. The action is denormalized to descriptor bounds, evaluated by the guarded surrogate, and converted to the next normalized target state and scalar reward. Thus, the 40-step horizon represents a sequence of policy-training queries rather than physical time evolution.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\stdfigure{fig2.pdf}
\caption{Workflow of DDPG as serialized static black-box search on a guarded surrogate. The process initializes a preference scenario, starts each episode from a random descriptor query, and then repeats absolute descriptor actions, guarded surrogate calls, state updates, and reward computation until the fixed query horizon is reached. The diagram describes a sequence of surrogate queries rather than physical time evolution or incremental morphology editing.}
\label{fig:ddpg-workflow}
\end{figure}

The scalar reward is computed from normalized target distance to the preference-specific ideal direction. EUIt is minimized, while EG and H are maximized. Let $y_i$ be the guarded surrogate output for target $i$, with $y_i^{\min}$ and $y_i^{\max}$ taken from the target bounds. The normalized target coordinate is


\begin{equation}
z_i =
\frac{y_i-y_i^{\min}}
{y_i^{\max}-y_i^{\min}} .
\end{equation}


The ideal target vector is


\begin{equation}
\bm u = [0,1,1]^\mathsf{T}.
\end{equation}


For preference weights $\bm w$, the weighted normalized distance is


\begin{equation}
d_w =
\frac{
\left\|
\bm w\odot(\bm z-\bm u)
\right\|_2}
{\left\|\bm w\right\|_2}.
\end{equation}


The training reward is therefore


\begin{equation}
R = 1 - d_w .
\end{equation}


This reward is separate from the fixed-domain post-hoc utility used later for benchmark comparison. The fixed-domain utility is a reporting metric, while $R$ is the scalar signal used during serialized DDPG training.

\subsection{Optimizer comparison protocol}

DDPG, NSGA-II, CMA-ES, RandomSearch, and FeasiblePoolRandom are represented through a retained-output contract before metrics are compared. The main manuscript emphasizes DDPG and NSGA-II because they define the policy-learning versus Pareto-search comparison. CMA-ES, RandomSearch, and FeasiblePoolRandom are retained as secondary Supplementary Information references for interpreting corner exploitation, uninformed search, and feasible-pool baselines.

All optimizer families use 20 seeds or runs and 24,000 surrogate queries per seed or run. DDPG retains one best scalarized candidate per seed, while NSGA-II retains a final population of 100 candidates per run. Equal-size HV and IGD are therefore reported at retained size 20 under benchmark-reference-v2, which avoids comparing a 20-candidate DDPG archive directly with a 2000-candidate NSGA-II archive. Oversized equal-size requests are labelled not applicable when the source archive is smaller than the requested retained size.

\subsection{Feasible projection, physics-based cross-model stress test, and cross-climate sensitivity}

Descriptor-space optimizer outputs are not assumed to be feasible blocks. Each retained descriptor-space candidate is therefore mapped to the nearest generated feasible block in normalized descriptor space. This mapping quantifies projection distance, duplicate collapse, and changes in HV and IGD under the fixed benchmark reference. The projection step is a representation check; it is not a physical simulation.

The limited physics-based cross-model stress test uses 24 locked cases. Eighteen direct feasible cases compare analytic response-generator values with physics-based outputs without optimizer projection, while six optimizer-linked cases decompose the path from descriptor-space candidate to nearest generated feasible block and then to physics-based result. The direct and optimizer-linked subsets answer different questions and are not merged into a single parity plot. The cross-climate sensitivity set then evaluates the same four block morphologies under Harbin, Beijing, and Guangzhou weather. The surrogate is not retrained, and the morphology set is not reselected after observing climate results.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Sampling coverage and surrogate robustness}

The generated 2000-row morphology pool covers a structured descriptor space rather than an unconstrained 12-dimensional design space. Principal component analysis shows that six components explain 95\% of descriptor variance. Descriptor-dependency checks further confirm that FAR equals BD times AF to numerical precision, OSR is linked to footprint and gross floor area, and OSLI remains integer-valued. These results justify reporting optimization outputs as descriptor-space candidates before feasible projection.

Across the three preference scenarios, DDPG reaches high-reward regions but does not behave as a monotonic physical improvement process. Each episode contains 40 surrogate interactions, and each scenario is trained for 600 episodes across 20 seeds. The best-to-final gap remains visible in the training summaries, which is consistent with a policy-training search process over a static response surface. The curves therefore describe optimizer behaviour under repeated surrogate calls rather than physical evolution of an urban block.

The descriptor-space result should be read with the HV ceiling in mind. NSGA-II covers the fixed reference volume almost completely, but this near-ceiling value does not by itself establish feasible-morphology diversity or physical ranking transfer. The retained-output contract and feasible projection results are therefore necessary before drawing design-support conclusions from the descriptor archive.

\subsection{Feasible morphology projection}

Feasible projection changes the representation of optimizer outputs. The NSGA-II descriptor archive contains 2000 retained candidates, but nearest-neighbour projection maps them to 51 unique generated feasible blocks, corresponding to a duplicate-collapse rate of 0.9745. Under the fixed benchmark reference, the NSGA-II archive changes from descriptor HV = 1.330999 and IGD = 0.004947 to projected feasible HV = 1.229611 and IGD = 0.156912. Balanced DDPG changes from descriptor HV = 0.661912 and IGD = 0.465522 to projected feasible HV = 0.990990 and IGD = 0.215410. These changes show that descriptor-space ranking and projected feasible ranking are not interchangeable.

\subsection{Physics-based cross-model stress test}

The physics-based cross-model stress test completed 24 locked cases. The 18 direct feasible cases are used for the main parity comparison because they compare analytic response-generator values with physics-based outputs without optimizer projection. For these direct cases, EUIt has nMAE = 0.340 and Spearman rho = -0.404, the GHI-based rooftop-PV proxy for EG has nMAE = 0.405 and Spearman rho = -0.023, and January 20 windowsill direct-sun hours for H has nMAE = 0.601 and Spearman rho = -0.043. Metric agreement is therefore weak, and ranking transfer is unsupported.

\subsection{Cross-climate sensitivity}

The limited four-block cross-climate physical sensitivity analysis evaluates selected morphologies under Harbin, Beijing, and Guangzhou weather relative to the Dongtai baseline. EG ordering remains stable across the three additional climates, with Spearman rho = 1.0 for all three stations. H ordering is unstable, with Spearman rho values of 0.2 for Beijing, 0.4 for Guangzhou, and 0.2 for Harbin. Severe-cold EUIt ordering is also unstable, with Harbin Spearman rho = 0.2.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Descriptor-space ranking and feasible morphology interpretation}

\rev{The descriptor-space comparison shows that NSGA-II is stronger than the three equal-size DDPG scenarios under benchmark-reference-v2, while the Generation-focused DDPG setting is the strongest of the three DDPG variants. This result is meaningful as a comparison of search behaviour in the selected surrogate environment. It should not be read as a direct ranking of feasible urban blocks, because the optimizer outputs first exist as continuous descriptor-space candidates.}

\rev{The projection analysis changes that interpretation. NSGA-II retains 2000 descriptor candidates, but these candidates map to 51 unique generated feasible blocks after nearest-neighbour projection. This compression shows that descriptor archive size and feasible-morphology diversity are different quantities. Projection distance and duplicate collapse are therefore necessary for interpreting continuous optimizer outputs in a morphology-generation workflow.}

\subsection{Physical and climate sensitivity boundaries}

\rev{The physics-based cross-model stress test completed all 24 locked cases, but the agreement between analytic target values and physics-based outputs is weak. For the 18 direct feasible cases, Spearman correlation is negative or near zero for EUIt, the EG proxy, and H. These results do not support transferring descriptor-space optimizer ranking to physics-based ranking. They instead define the degree of mismatch between the analytic response surface and the tested physical workflows.}

\rev{The cross-climate sensitivity analysis provides a limited view of weather dependence. EG ordering remains stable across Harbin, Beijing, and Guangzhou for the four selected blocks, whereas H ordering is unstable and EUIt ordering becomes unstable in the severe-cold Harbin case. The surrogate is not retrained across climates, so these results should be interpreted as physical sensitivity of selected morphologies rather than as cross-climate surrogate generalization.}

\subsection{Implications, limitations, and future work}

For early-stage urban energy design, the workflow is useful because it separates fast descriptor-space screening from feasible-block projection and physical sensitivity analysis. This separation is important for design practice. A surrogate optimizer can identify promising descriptor regions, but generated feasible blocks and physics-based checks remain necessary before drawing design conclusions.

Several limitations remain. The optimizer benchmark is conducted on an analytic response-generation dataset rather than on direct annual physical simulation for all generated morphologies. The 12 surrogate inputs are descriptors derived from generated blocks and do not form an independent unconstrained design space. The DDPG environment serializes queries to a static surrogate and should not be interpreted as a physical-time control problem. The physics-based cross-model stress test shows weak ranking transfer, and the climate analysis covers only four blocks and three additional climates. Future work should connect the optimizer to a feasible morphology decoder, expand the physical simulation set, incorporate storage, EV charging, and distributed-energy interactions, and retrain surrogates across multiple climate regions.

\section{Conclusions}

This study examined policy learning and Pareto search in a surrogate-assisted workflow for block-scale urban energy design. The workflow first generates feasible block morphologies, converts them into 12 morphology descriptors, assigns analytic EUIt, EG, and H targets, and trains a selected DNN surrogate from the 2000-sample regime of a nested scale study.

The first conclusion is that the surrogate is accurate within the analytic response-generation regime but remains tied to the sampled descriptor domain. Repeated cross-validation gives low nMAE for all three targets, while holdout assessments and descriptor-dependency checks show that the input space is a structured morphology-derived descriptor space rather than an independent continuous design space.

The second conclusion is that optimizer ranking changes when descriptor-space candidates are projected to generated feasible morphologies. NSGA-II reaches near-ceiling descriptor-space HV under the fixed benchmark reference, but its 2000 descriptor candidates contract to 51 unique feasible blocks after projection. This projection result prevents descriptor-space archive size from being interpreted as feasible-morphology diversity.

The third conclusion is that physical and climate sensitivity analyses restrict the scope of interpretation. The 24-case physics-based cross-model stress test completed without failed cases, but metric agreement and ranking transfer are weak. The four-block cross-climate sensitivity analysis shows stable EG ordering but unstable H ordering and severe-cold EUIt ordering. These results support explicit reporting of projection distance, model agreement, and climate sensitivity whenever surrogate-conditioned optimizer rankings are used for early-stage urban energy design.
