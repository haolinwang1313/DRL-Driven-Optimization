\begin{abstract}
\rev{Block-scale urban energy design requires methods that relate urban morphology to demand, rooftop photovoltaic potential, and winter sunlight access while controlling the cost of simulation-based search. This study evaluates how optimizer ranking changes when policy learning and Pareto search operate on the same surrogate response surface. Feasible block morphologies are generated first, converted into 12 morphology descriptors, and assigned EUIt, EG, and H values through an analytic demand-generation-sunlight response generator. The selected DNN surrogate is trained on the 2000-sample regime identified from a nested 500/1000/1500/2000-sample scale study and then used as the shared environment for DDPG and NSGA-II. Repeated cross-validation gives mean nMAE values of 0.0176, 0.0265, and 0.0145 for EUIt, EG, and H. In descriptor space, NSGA-II attains near-ceiling HV under the canonical reference, whereas equal-size DDPG results remain scenario dependent. After nearest-feasible-block projection, the large NSGA-II descriptor archive contracts from 2000 candidates to 51 unique feasible blocks, and projected rankings differ from descriptor-space rankings. A 24-case physics-based cross-model stress test shows weak metric agreement and unsupported ranking transfer, while a four-block cross-climate sensitivity analysis shows stable EG ordering but unstable H ordering and severe-cold EUIt ordering. The results support a design-support interpretation: surrogate-conditioned optimizer rankings should be reported with validation mode, projection distance, and climate sensitivity rather than interpreted as direct feasible-space or physics-based superiority.}
\end{abstract}

\section{\rev{Introduction}}

\subsection{\rev{Block-scale urban energy design under surrogate conditioning}}

\rev{Block-scale urban morphology affects building energy demand, rooftop solar access, ventilation exposure, and winter sunlight availability. These effects are relevant to urban energy planning because early-stage morphology decisions constrain later choices in building systems, distributed photovoltaic deployment, and demand-side flexibility. Simulation-based optimization can connect these decisions, yet full annual energy and solar simulations remain costly when thousands of candidate blocks and multiple optimizer runs must be compared. Surrogate-assisted workflows therefore remain useful as design-support tools, provided that their ranking limits are stated explicitly \cite{ref1,ref2,ref6,ref7,ref8}.}

\rev{The central difficulty is not only surrogate prediction accuracy. Once an optimizer searches directly on a surrogate response surface, method ranking can depend on the sampled descriptor domain, the surrogate checkpoint, the output clipping rule, and the way continuous descriptor outputs are interpreted as feasible urban blocks. A method that performs well in descriptor space may still map to a small number of generated feasible morphologies or may fail to preserve ordering under a physics-based cross-model check. Consequently, optimizer comparison in this setting must report not only objective values, but also the representation in which those values are computed.}

\subsection{\rev{Policy learning and Pareto search in static morphology optimization}}

\rev{Evolutionary multi-objective optimizers, especially NSGA-II, remain standard tools for building and urban energy design because they generate explicit trade-off archives \cite{ref11,ref12,ref13,ref16,ref17,ref22,ref23,ref24,ref25}. Policy-learning methods provide a different search mechanism. They can encode preference-conditioned scalar rewards and repeatedly query a continuous surrogate environment \cite{ref26,ref31,ref32}. In the present problem, however, the DDPG formulation does not represent a natural physical-time control process. It serializes repeated queries to a static surrogate response surface. Each action is an absolute normalized 12-descriptor vector, and each episode is a fixed sequence of 40 surrogate queries. This distinction is essential for interpreting the role of DDPG in the study.}

\rev{Recent urban-form and building-energy studies show that morphology affects energy demand, photovoltaic potential, and daylight access across a range of scales \cite{ref3,ref4,ref5,ref41,ref42,ref43}. Surrogate modelling can reduce evaluation cost in these settings \cite{ref18,ref19,ref20}, while reinforcement-learning studies in energy systems mainly address operational control rather than static morphology generation \cite{ref33,ref34,ref35,ref36,ref37,ref38,ref39}. The present study therefore does not claim a general ranking of policy learning against evolutionary search. It asks a narrower question: how should optimizer rankings be reported when DDPG and NSGA-II share the same controlled analytic response surface and the same surrogate-query budget?}

\subsection{\rev{Contributions and paper organization}}

\rev{This study contributes a bounded method comparison for surrogate-assisted block-scale urban energy design.}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \rev{It constructs a feasible morphology generation workflow in which block geometries are generated before 12 morphology descriptors are calculated, thereby separating feasible block construction from continuous descriptor-space optimization.}
\item \rev{It trains and assesses a DNN surrogate on an analytic demand-generation-sunlight response surface selected from nested 500/1000/1500/2000-sample regimes, with repeated validation and grouped holdout assessments.}
\item \rev{It compares DDPG and NSGA-II under a shared surrogate environment and matched surrogate-query budgets, while distinguishing retained descriptor archives, equal-size archives, and projected feasible morphology archives.}
\item \rev{It maps continuous optimizer outputs to generated feasible blocks and quantifies projection distance, duplicate collapse, and ranking changes before interpreting optimizer outputs as design-support candidates.}
\item \rev{It examines 24 physics-based case runs and 12 additional climate runs to define the interpretation boundary between analytic response ranking, projected feasible morphology ranking, and physics-based sensitivity.}
\end{enumerate}

\rev{The remainder of the manuscript is organized as follows. Section~2 describes feasible morphology generation, analytic response generation, surrogate assessment, serialized DDPG search, the optimizer comparison protocol, and the physical and climate sensitivity checks. Section~3 reports sampling coverage, surrogate robustness, DDPG training behaviour, the canonical DDPG and NSGA-II comparison, feasible projection, physics-based cross-model stress-test results, and cross-climate sensitivity. Section~4 interprets the result scope for design-support use, and Section~5 gives the conclusions. Supplementary Information Sections S1--S6 provide the case settings, equations, additional surrogate assessments, optimizer details, projection rows, per-case physical results, and climate tables.}

\subsection{\rev{Feasible morphology generation and descriptor construction}}

\rev{The morphology generator first constructs feasible block layouts and only then calculates descriptor values. Each block is represented as a 240~m $\times$ 240~m square divided into a $3\times3$ grid of 80~m $\times$ 80~m land units. One cell is assigned as open space, and the remaining eight cells are populated with residential prototypes. For each generated block, the generator samples the open-space position, building prototype, number of floors, and orientation angle. The orientation angle is sampled within -45~deg to 45~deg. The resulting block is therefore a generated feasible morphology, while the 12 input quantities used by the surrogate are descriptors derived from that morphology.}

\rev{The descriptor set includes FAR, SD, AF, AR\_ew, AR\_ns, SVF, BD, OSR, SC, PAR, theta, and OSLI. These quantities are not 12 independent design variables. For example, FAR, BD, AF, and OSR are linked through the same footprint and gross-floor-area quantities, while OSLI is an integer descriptor of open-space position. Consequently, optimization results are reported as descriptor-space candidates until they are projected back to generated feasible blocks.}

\subsection{\rev{Analytic response generation}}

\rev{The target values in the optimizer benchmark come from an analytic response generator rather than from direct annual EnergyPlus and Radiance execution.
The analytic mapping converts the 12 morphology descriptors to EUIt, EG, and H through explicit demand, rooftop-generation, and sunlight-access functions. These functions preserve the intended directional interpretation of the original physics-based workflow: lower EUIt is preferred, while higher EG and H are preferred. The active 2000-sample regime is a nested prefix of the maximum generated morphology pool and uses simulation\_mode = fallback\_analytic.}

\rev{This choice makes the benchmark reproducible and computationally stable, but it also defines the main interpretation boundary of the paper. The surrogate and optimizer comparison assess behaviour on a controlled analytic response surface. They do not replace full annual physical simulation of every generated morphology. The Dongtai station has zero fallback bias in the analytic generator, while non-Dongtai fallback bias is not used for the main benchmark. Supplementary Section S1 gives the full equations, station-bias convention, Gaussian-noise terms, and clipping ranges of 66.0--96.0 for EUIt, 1.2--2.85 for EG, and 6.0--7.85 for H.}

\subsection{\rev{Surrogate model training and robustness assessment}}

\rev{The selected surrogate is assessed through repeated five-fold cross-validation, leave-one-OSLI-out validation, outer-shell holdout, and feature-tail holdout. The repeated five-fold protocol evaluates interpolation performance under randomized folds. The leave-one-OSLI-out protocol tests whether the surrogate remains stable when one open-space-position class is withheld. The outer-shell and feature-tail holdouts stress the descriptor-domain boundary without claiming true out-of-distribution generalization.}

\rev{The selected DNN comes from the 2000-sample regime of the nested scale study. The round-2 assessment keeps the model-comparison role separate from the optimizer-comparison role: the scale study identifies the surrogate checkpoint, while the robustness checks report how accurately that checkpoint reproduces the analytic response-generation regime. The repeated cross-validation mean nMAE values are 0.0176 for EUIt, 0.0265 for EG, and 0.0145 for H, with corresponding Spearman correlations of 0.991, 0.984, and 0.995.}

\subsection{\rev{DDPG as serialized static black-box search}}

\rev{The DDPG environment serializes repeated queries to a static guarded surrogate. At the beginning of each episode, a random descriptor query is evaluated to construct the initial normalized three-target state. At each subsequent step, the actor outputs an absolute 12-dimensional normalized descriptor action. This action is not an incremental modification of the preceding block. The action is denormalized to descriptor bounds, evaluated by the guarded surrogate, and converted to the next normalized target state and scalar reward. Thus, the 40-step horizon represents a sequence of policy-training queries rather than physical time evolution.}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\stdfigure{fig2.pdf}
\caption{\rev{Workflow of DDPG as serialized static black-box search on a guarded surrogate. The process initializes a preference scenario, starts each episode from a random descriptor query, and then repeats absolute descriptor actions, guarded surrogate calls, state updates, and reward computation until the fixed query horizon is reached. The diagram describes a sequence of surrogate queries rather than physical time evolution or incremental morphology editing.}}
\label{fig:ddpg-workflow}
\end{figure}

\rev{The scalar reward is computed from normalized target distance to the preference-specific ideal direction. EUIt is minimized, while EG and H are maximized. Let $y_i$ be the guarded surrogate output for target $i$, with $y_i^{\min}$ and $y_i^{\max}$ taken from the target bounds. The normalized target coordinate is}

\begin{revblock}
\begin{equation}
z_i =
\frac{y_i-y_i^{\min}}
{y_i^{\max}-y_i^{\min}} .
\end{equation}
\end{revblock}

\rev{The ideal target vector is}

\begin{revblock}
\begin{equation}
\bm u = [0,1,1]^\mathsf{T}.
\end{equation}
\end{revblock}

\rev{For preference weights $\bm w$, the weighted normalized distance is}

\begin{revblock}
\begin{equation}
d_w =
\frac{
\left\|
\bm w\odot(\bm z-\bm u)
\right\|_2}
{\left\|\bm w\right\|_2}.
\end{equation}
\end{revblock}

\rev{The training reward is therefore}

\begin{revblock}
\begin{equation}
R = 1 - d_w .
\end{equation}
\end{revblock}

\rev{This reward is separate from the fixed-domain post-hoc utility used later for benchmark comparison. The fixed-domain utility is a reporting metric, while $R$ is the scalar signal used during serialized DDPG training.}

\subsection{\rev{Optimizer comparison protocol}}

\rev{DDPG, NSGA-II, CMA-ES, RandomSearch, and FeasiblePoolRandom are represented through a retained-output contract before metrics are compared. The main manuscript emphasizes DDPG and NSGA-II because they define the policy-learning versus Pareto-search comparison. CMA-ES, RandomSearch, and FeasiblePoolRandom are retained as secondary Supplementary Information references for interpreting corner exploitation, uninformed search, and feasible-pool baselines.}

\rev{All optimizer families use 20 seeds or runs and 24,000 surrogate queries per seed or run. DDPG retains one best scalarized candidate per seed, while NSGA-II retains a final population of 100 candidates per run. Equal-size HV and IGD are therefore reported at retained size 20 under benchmark-reference-v2, which avoids comparing a 20-candidate DDPG archive directly with a 2000-candidate NSGA-II archive. \subsection{\rev{Feasible projection, physics-based cross-model stress test, and cross-climate sensitivity}}

\rev{Descriptor-space optimizer outputs are not assumed to be feasible blocks. Each retained descriptor-space candidate is therefore mapped to the nearest generated feasible block in normalized descriptor space. This mapping quantifies projection distance, duplicate collapse, and changes in HV and IGD under the fixed benchmark reference. The projection step is a representation check; it is not a physical simulation.}

\rev{The limited physics-based cross-model stress test uses 24 locked cases. Eighteen direct feasible cases compare analytic response-generator values with physics-based outputs without optimizer projection, while six optimizer-linked cases decompose the path from descriptor-space candidate to nearest generated feasible block and then to physics-based result. The direct and optimizer-linked subsets answer different questions and are not merged into a single parity plot. The cross-climate sensitivity set then evaluates the same four block morphologies under Harbin, Beijing, and Guangzhou weather. The surrogate is not retrained, and the morphology set is not reselected after observing climate results.}

\section{\rev{Results}}

\subsection{\rev{Sampling coverage and surrogate robustness}}

\rev{The generated 2000-row morphology pool covers a structured descriptor space rather than an unconstrained 12-dimensional design space. Principal component analysis shows that six components explain 95\% of descriptor variance. Descriptor-dependency checks further confirm that FAR equals BD times AF to numerical precision, OSR is linked to footprint and gross floor area, and OSLI remains integer-valued. These results justify reporting optimization outputs as descriptor-space candidates before feasible projection.}

The retained-output contract and feasible projection results are therefore necessary before drawing design-support conclusions from the descriptor archive.}

\subsection{\rev{Feasible-morphology projection}}

\rev{Feasible projection changes the representation of optimizer outputs. The NSGA-II descriptor archive contains 2000 retained candidates, but nearest-neighbour projection maps them to 51 unique generated feasible blocks, corresponding to a duplicate-collapse rate of 0.9745. Under the fixed benchmark reference, the NSGA-II archive changes from descriptor HV = 1.330999 and IGD = 0.004947 to projected feasible HV = 1.229611 and IGD = 0.156912. Balanced DDPG changes from descriptor HV = 0.661912 and IGD = 0.465522 to projected feasible HV = 0.990990 and IGD = 0.215410. These changes show that descriptor-space ranking and projected feasible ranking are not interchangeable.}

\section{\rev{Discussion}}

\subsection{\rev{What the descriptor-space comparison supports}}

\rev{The descriptor-space comparison supports a bounded method-ranking statement under the selected analytic response surface and benchmark-reference-v2. NSGA-II is stronger than the three equal-size DDPG scenarios in HV and IGD, and the Generation-focused DDPG scenario is the strongest of the three DDPG settings in descriptor space. This result is useful for comparing search mechanisms under a shared surrogate environment, but it should not be interpreted as a direct ranking of feasible urban blocks under physical simulation.}

\subsection{\rev{Why feasible projection changes interpretation}}

\rev{Feasible projection is not a minor formatting step. It changes both archive size and objective summaries because continuous descriptor-space candidates may map to a much smaller set of generated feasible blocks. The NSGA-II archive illustrates this effect most clearly: 2000 descriptor candidates contract to 51 unique generated feasible blocks after nearest-neighbour projection. Therefore, descriptor-space archive richness cannot be treated as feasible-morphology diversity unless projection distance and duplicate collapse are reported.}

\subsection{\rev{Physical and climatic interpretation boundaries}}

\rev{The physics-based cross-model stress test and cross-climate sensitivity analysis restrict rather than strengthen the optimizer-ranking claim. The stress test completed all locked cases, but metric agreement is weak and ranking transfer is unsupported. The climate analysis shows that EG ordering is stable across the tested climates, while H ordering and severe-cold EUIt ordering are unstable. These results are valuable because they identify where the analytic response surface does not transfer cleanly to physical or weather-sensitive outputs.}

\subsection{\rev{Implications for urban energy design workflows}}

\rev{For early-stage urban energy design, the practical implication is not to abandon surrogate-assisted optimization. The implication is to report the representation and validation mode of each result. A descriptor-space optimizer comparison can help screen search mechanisms and preference scenarios. A feasible-morphology projection can identify whether continuous outputs collapse to a small feasible set. A physics-based stress test can reveal whether analytic rankings remain stable under different model assumptions. A climate sensitivity analysis can show whether selected morphologies remain robust when weather changes. Used together, these steps define a design-support workflow rather than a physical optimizer certification.}

\subsection{\rev{Limitations and future work}}

\rev{Several limitations remain. First, the optimizer benchmark is conducted on an analytic response-generation dataset rather than on direct annual physical simulations for all generated morphologies. Second, the 12 surrogate inputs are descriptors derived from generated blocks and do not form an independent unconstrained design space. Third, the DDPG environment serializes queries to a static surrogate and should not be interpreted as a natural physical-time control problem. Fourth, the physics-based cross-model stress test shows weak metric agreement and unsupported ranking transfer. Fifth, the cross-climate analysis covers four blocks and three additional climates, so it should be interpreted as sensitivity analysis rather than general transferability. Future work should connect the optimizer directly to a feasible morphology decoder, expand the physical simulation set, incorporate dynamic energy-system components with storage and EV charging loads, and retrain surrogates across multiple climate regions.}

\section{\rev{Conclusions}}

\rev{This study examined policy learning and Pareto search in a surrogate-assisted workflow for block-scale urban energy design. The workflow first generates feasible block morphologies, converts them into 12 morphology descriptors, assigns analytic EUIt, EG, and H targets, and trains a selected DNN surrogate from the 2000-sample regime of a nested scale study.}

\rev{The first conclusion is that the surrogate is accurate within the analytic response-generation regime but remains tied to the sampled descriptor domain. Repeated cross-validation gives low nMAE for all three targets, while holdout assessments and descriptor-dependency checks show that the input space is a structured morphology-derived descriptor space rather than an independent continuous design space.}

\rev{The second conclusion is that optimizer ranking changes when descriptor-space candidates are projected to generated feasible morphologies. NSGA-II reaches near-ceiling descriptor-space HV under the canonical reference, but its 2000 descriptor candidates contract to 51 unique feasible blocks after projection. This projection result prevents descriptor-space archive size from being interpreted as feasible-morphology diversity.}

\rev{The third conclusion is that physical and climatic sensitivity analyses restrict the scope of interpretation. The 24-case physics-based cross-model stress test completed without failed cases, but metric agreement and ranking transfer are weak. The four-block cross-climate sensitivity analysis shows stable EG ordering but unstable H ordering and severe-cold EUIt ordering. These results support explicit reporting of projection distance, model agreement, and climate sensitivity whenever surrogate-conditioned optimizer rankings are used for early-stage urban energy design.}
